# Week 3: Frames And Distance Management

## Class Goal

Students build reliable frames before trying to escape.

## Concepts

1. Frames.
2. Distance management.

## Memory Phrase

No frame, no space.

## Whiteboard Content

1. Decision rule under pressure: build two functional frames before any escape movement.
2. Frames: bone-supported barriers that create or preserve space so you can defend and move safely.
3. Arrow cue: if wrist to elbow is an arrow, keep that arrow pointing toward your near knee or near hip line so the frame stays supported.
4. Space rule: frames plus movement create space; frames without movement mostly delay pressure.
5. Basic movement options for creating space: hip escape or bridge.
6. Distance management: controlling how close or far your opponent is so you decide when to defend, move, or attack.
7. Distance target: make enough space to insert knee or hip movement, not enough space to let the top player reset control.

Why?

1. Bones do not fatigue. Muscle-based pushing drains energy quickly, but skeletal alignment holds indefinitely.
2. Proper frame alignment redirects opponent's weight into the mat instead of absorbing it in your arms.
3. Frames buy decision-making time. Without frames, pressure forces panic reactions.
4. Controlled distance gives you movement options without giving your opponent space to reset grips or re-engage.

## Suggested Teaching Wording

Opening: Frames are your first safety tool under pressure.
Teaching point: Build structure first, then move. Frames are bone-supported barriers, not arm wrestling.

### Core Script

First concept is frames. A frame is a strong structure made with bone alignment, not arm strength. In bottom side control, use one forearm frame at the near shoulder or jawline/collarbone line and one frame at the near hip line. Keep the neck-frame elbow inside your structure, connected through your torso toward your near knee, so the frame has support and does not collapse.

Use this visual once the frames are placed: if wrist to elbow is an arrow, keep that arrow pointed toward your near knee or near hip line. That keeps your elbow inside and supported.

Second concept is distance management. Your goal is not to create huge space. Your goal is to create enough safe space to move. Too little space and you are pinned. Too much space and you lose control of the exchange.

Important distinction: frames alone usually do not create lasting space. Frames plus a small bridge, shoulder turn, or hip escape create space. Then your frames preserve that space long enough to recover position.

Use a simple range check: if your knee can start to enter and your hips can move, distance is enough. If the top player can immediately collapse back chest-to-chest, distance was too small. If they fully disengage and reset grips, distance was too big.

Watch this rep: I build frame one, then frame two, then I move my hips. I do not move first. If I move first, I get flattened. If I frame first, I can move safely.

Now the common mistake: bent arms and pushing with muscle. That burns energy and your frames collapse.
Correction: straighten the frame structure, point your wrist-to-elbow arrow toward your near knee line, and hold position before moving.

## Drill

Drill 1: Full Sequence Flow.

Setup:

1. Start in side control bottom.
2. Build two frames before any movement attempt.
3. Run slow, clean reps before adding resistance.
4. On each rep, pause for one second and check distance before moving.

## Concept Transfer To Other Positions

1. Bottom mount: frame on hips and biceps before bridge attempts.
2. Guard retention: frames buy time to reinsert knee and hook positions.
3. Standing ties: frame on shoulders or biceps to manage distance safely.
4. In every position, manage range the same way: create enough space to improve position, not so much space that you lose control.
